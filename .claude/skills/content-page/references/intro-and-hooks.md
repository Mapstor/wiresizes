# Intro formula & hooks

The intro decides whether the visitor scrolls or bounces — and bounces are
bad clicks. Every intro follows pain → stakes → promise, sized to the
archetype.

## Article / guide pages — the full formula

Three beats, three short paragraphs, nothing else before the answer block:

1. **Pain** — name the exact situation that produced the search, in the
   reader's own words. ("You know you need an AC. But how big? 2 tons? 3?
   What do BTUs even mean for your house?") Use the deep-dive's question
   phrasings — they ARE the reader's words.
2. **Stakes** — why getting it wrong costs something real, anchored by a
   verified statistic. ("Oversized units short-cycle: they cost more up
   front, wear out faster, and dehumidify worse.") **The stat rule: the
   number comes from the data-sourcing registry with its attribution, or the
   stakes are stated without a number.** Never invent the hook stat — it is
   the first thing raters, reviewers, and readers see.
3. **Promise** — what this page delivers and the only-here wedge. ("Below:
   the actual sizing math, a calculator that does it for your inputs, and
   the mistake most sizing charts make.") Preview the tool/wedge explicitly
   — don't bury the lead.

Then the 40–60-word direct-answer block. The formula never delays the
answer; it frames it.

## Tool / map pages — the compressed hook

Two lines maximum above the tool: one line of pain + promise fused ("Sizing
an AC wrong costs you twice — get the right BTU number in 30 seconds"), one
line of credibility ("Based on [primary source] data, methodology below").
The full pain/stakes/promise material moves BELOW the tool as the opening of
the prose section. The tool is the lead; never push it below the fold with
prose.

## Data / reference pages

Pain and promise fuse into a single orienting paragraph + the answer block:
what this data is, why the reader cares, what's unique here. Stakes only if
a real number supports them.

## Hook quality tests

- Would the person who typed the primary keyword nod at the first sentence?
- Is every number in the intro registry-verified with a working source link?
- Does the promise name the wedge specifically (not "everything you need to
  know")?
- Three paragraphs or fewer before the answer block?
- Zero throat-clearing ("In today's world…", "When it comes to…") — delete
  on sight.

## Voice

Conversational-expert throughout: "you" for the reader, "we" for the site.
Contractions welcome. Short declarative sentences. Confidence comes from
sources, not adjectives — cut "amazing", "incredible", "game-changing".
Skepticism of rules of thumb is on-brand ("most charts use a shortcut; here
is the actual calculation") when the criticism is accurate and sourced.
